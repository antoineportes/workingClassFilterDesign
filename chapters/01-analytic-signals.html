    Euler's formula

    Let $ \; j \, = \, \sqrt{-1} \; $ and $ \; x \; $ be a real number, then Euler 's formula states that:

    $$ \exp $$$$ (jx) \; = \; \cos(x) \, + \, j\sin(x) $$

    ↑↑↑ Don't overlook that one. ↑↑↑

    We'll demonstrate it at some point:

    • Chapter v Poles and zeroes, Appendix Taylor series
    • Chapter vii Stephen Butterworth, Appendix Post-apocalupsis trigonometry

    By making use of the two following trigonometric identities :

    $$ \cos(-x) \; = \; \cos(x) $$

    and

    $$ \sin(-x) \; = \; -\sin(x) $$

    Euler's formula can be re-expressed as:

    $$ \exp(j \cdot -x) \; = \; \cos(-x) \: + \: j\sin(-x) \; = \; \cos(x) \: - \: j\sin(x) $$

    Then:

    $$ \exp(jx) \, - \, \exp(-jx) \; = \; \cos(x) \, + \, j\sin(x) \, - \, \bigl( \cos(x) \, - \, j\sin(x) \bigr) $$ $$ \hphantom{\exp(jx) \, - \, \exp(-jx) \;} =\; 2j\sin(x) $$

    and:

    $$ \exp(jx) \, + \, \exp(-jx) \; = \; \cos(x) \, + \, j\sin(x) \, + \, \bigl( \cos(x) \, - \, j\sin(x) \bigr) $$ $$ \hphantom{\exp(jx) \, - \, \exp(-jx) \;} =\; 2\cos(x) $$

    Solving [1] $$ \exp(jx) \, - \, \exp(-jx) \; = \; 2j\sin(x) $$ for $ \; \sin(x) \; $ gives:

    $$ \sin(x) \; = \; \frac{ \exp(jx) \, - \, \exp(-jx) }{2j} $$

    Solving [2] $$ \exp(jx) \, + \, \exp(-jx) \; = \; 2\cos(x) $$ for $ \; \cos(x) \; $ gives:

    $$ \cos(x) \; = \; \frac{ \exp(jx) \, + \, \exp(-jx) }{2} $$

    Considering the identity:

    $$ \cos(\theta) \; = \; \sin(\theta \, + \, \pi/2) $$

    θ ("theta"), not 0 ("zero")

    equation [3] $$ \cos(x) \; = \; \frac{ \exp(jx)\, + \exp(-jx) }{2} $$ shows that any real sinusoid can be expressed as half the sum of two complex exponentials.

    This is interesting because the rules of exponentiation are a lot simpler than the rules of trigonometry.

    For instance, $ \; \exp(x) \; $ is its own derivative (and thus anti-derivative) — by definition.

    More on that soon enough...

    This makes processes such as integration or differentiation become very trivial, compared to the same processes applied to $ \; \cos(x) \; $ or $ \; \sin(x) \, $.

    (Actually, derivatives and antiderivatives are not where real trigonometric functions are the trickiest, but: $ \; \cos(ax \, + \, b) \; $, &c.)

    Let:

    $$ z_1 \; = \; \exp(jx)/2 $$

    and:

    $$ z_2 \; = \; \exp(-jx)/2 $$

    then:

    $$ z_1 \; = \; \cos(x)/2 \, + \, j \cdot +\sin(x)/2 $$ $$ z_2 \; = \; \cos(x)/2 \, + \, j \cdot -\sin(x)/2 $$

    Their respective real ($ \Re $) and imaginary ($ \Im $) parts are thus:

    $$ \Re(z_1) \; = \; \cos(x)/2 $$

    ;

    $$ \Im(z_1) \; = \; +\sin(x)/2 $$     $$ \Re(z_2) \; = \; \cos(x)/2 $$

    ;

    $$ \Im(z_2) \; = \; -\sin(x)/2 $$     $$ \implies\; \left\{ \; \begin{alignedat}{1} &\Re(z_1) \; = \; \Re(z_2) \\[0.5em] &\Im(z_1) \; = \; -\Im(z_2) \end{alignedat} \right. $$

    $ z_1 $ and $ z_2 $ form a complex conjugate pair.
    (Which means: they have equal real parts and opposite imaginary parts.)

    So any real sinusoid (equation [3] $$ \cos(x) \; = \; \frac{ \exp(jx)\, + \exp(-jx) }{2} $$ ) can be expressed as the sum of two complex conjugates.

    Complex conjugates are usually denoted by a horizontal bar over the number's symbol:

    $$ z_2 \; = \; \overline{z_1} $$

    means that z₂ and z₁ are conjugates.

    Sometimes also with an asterisk:

    $$ z_1 \; = \; {z_2}^* $$

    First option is really not convenient because monospace (... GitHub's the original
    — you're reading the derivative...)     .

    Second option looks like multiplication in most programming languages — or convolution .

    So I'll use:

    $$ z_2 \; = \; \conj(z_1) $$

    — the Desmos way, for practical reasons.

    But know that this is not standard and be ready to encounter other conventions.

    Euler's formula and the polar form

    Let's consider a complex number $ z $ with argument $ \; \arg(z) \; $ and absolute value $ \; \left| z \right| \; $.

    According to Euler's formula:

    $$ \exp\bigl( j\arg(z) \bigr) \; = \; \cos\bigl( \arg(z) \bigr) \, + \, j\sin\bigl( \arg(z) \bigr) $$

    Recalling the polar form expression:

    $$ z \; = \; \left| z \right| \Bigl( \: \cos\bigl( \arg(z) \bigr) \, + \, j\sin\bigl( \arg(z) \bigr) \: \Bigr) $$

    Combining these two expressions leads to:

    $$ z \; = \; \left| z \right| \exp\bigl( j\arg(z) \bigr) $$

    I haven't found any documentation on this form (perhaps from not digging deep enough...) so let's call it the exponential form of the complex number $ z $.

    Edit:

    I've dug, eventually — and it turns out that this guess was a lucky one.
    I almost never say this, but: you can safely quote me here.

    This means that any complex number in the form:

    $$ \zeta \; = \; \alpha\exp(j\theta) $$

    θ ("theta"), not 0 ("zero")

    has an absolute value: $ \; \left| \zeta \right| \: = \: \alpha \; $ and an argument: $ \; \arg(\zeta) \: = \: \theta \; $.

    Then:

    $$ \Re(\zeta) \; = \; \alpha\cos(\theta) $$

    and:

    $$ \Im(\zeta) \; = \; \alpha\sin(\theta) $$

    Now in the special case: $ \; \theta \: = \: \omega t \; $, where $ t $ is the time (in seconds) and $ \omega $ is an angular frequency (in radians per second), the exponential form:

    $$ \zeta \; = \; \alpha\exp(j\omega t) $$

    is what is called a complex sinusoid.

    It's also called an analytic signal as it has optimal qualities in order to study a system's response to pure sinusoids.
    Indeed comparing equations [3] $$ \cos(x) \; = \; \frac{ \exp(jx)\, + \exp(-jx) }{2} $$ and [4] $$ \zeta \; = \; \alpha\exp(j\omega t) $$ reveals that if we express a real sinusoid in terms of complex exponentials, we end up with two complex sinusoids, one of which has a negative frequency; while a pure complex sinusoid has its amplitude and phase embedded in its constant factor and only involves positive frequencies.
    More on that right away.

    Edit:

    It's the chapter's title... better be slightly more precise.

    A complex sinusoid is an analytic signal only if $ \omega $ is positive.

    That's the complete definition:

    complex-valued function
    and no negative-frequency component.

    Starting with a real cosine, it can be thought of in two ways:

    • add a $ \pi/2 $ shifted copy of the signal to itself on the imaginary axis;
    • double the positive-frequency component, remove the negative-frequency one.

    Both operations result in the substitution:

    $$ \cos(\omega t) \quad\longleftarrow\quad \cos(\omega t) \, + \, j\sin(\omega t) \; = \; \exp(j\omega t) $$

    More details → here ←.

    It's also pretty convenient when testing trigonometric identities, because it embeds the three types of sinusoids.

    E.g.:

    $$ \exp\bigl( j( x \, + \, n\pi ) \bigr) \; = \; \exp(jx)\exp(j\pi)^n $$ $$ \hphantom{\exp\bigl( j( x \, + \, n\pi ) \bigr) \;} = \; (-1)^n\cdot\exp(jx) $$ $$ \hphantom{\exp\bigl( j( x \, + \, n\pi ) \bigr) \;} = \; (-1)^n\cdot \Bigl( \cos(x) + j\sin(x) \Bigr) $$ $$ \hphantom{\exp\bigl( j( x \, + \, n\pi ) \bigr) \;} = \; (-1)^n\cdot\cos(x) \, + \, j\cdot(-1)^n\cdot\sin(x) $$ $$ \implies\; \left\{ \begin{alignedat}{1} \cos(x \, + \, n\pi) \; = \; (-1)^n\cdot\cos(x) \\[0.5em] \sin(x \, + \, n\pi) \; = \; (-1)^n\cdot\sin(x) \end{alignedat} \right. $$

    Before we move on: what happens to $ \zeta $ if $ \alpha $ has an imaginary part?

    $$ \alpha \; = \; \left| \alpha \right| \exp\bigl( j\arg(\alpha) \bigr) $$

    then:

    $$ \zeta \; = \; \left| \alpha \right| \exp\bigl( j\arg(\alpha) \bigr) \exp(j\omega t) $$ $$ \hphantom{\zeta\;} = \; \left| \alpha \right| \exp\Bigl(\, j\bigl( \omega t \, + \, \arg(\alpha) \bigr)\, \Bigr) $$

    Fourier starts getting the serious attention he deserves in section 07.

    Joseph Fourier, in Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822), basically demonstrated that any signal can be decomposed into a sum of sinusoids of various amplitudes, frequencies and phases.

    These sinusoids are called partials and their sum is called spectrum.

    Edit — woops:

    That's not what a spectrum is (forgive me, I thought so for years).

    Sinusoids are the time-domain representation of the signal.
    Spectrum is a frequency-domain concept: it's the amplitude and phase per frequency band of a signal.

    I'm not sure that what I've erroneously called a spectrum thus far has any other name than just "signal"...

    Except: representing it as a sum of sinusoids is in fact making use of its spectral data.

    Pardon the long interruption but I struggle at seeing frequency and time domains as separate hermetic entities — theoretically, Bartók's Concerto for orchestra is one single waveform — I guess my ears suck at maths then.

    Frequency without time is a division by zero.

    If you sort your underwear by colour, the colours don't start having a separate autonomous existence.

    The equal signs in the Laplace and Fourier transforms are not figures of speech.

    If all partials have their frequencies related by an integer factor, they are called harmonics.
    Then the one with the (non-zero) lowest frequency is called fundamental and all the other ones are called overtones.

    In such a case, the composite signal is periodic and its frequency is the fundamental's frequency, thus called fundamental frequency.

    In case of a periodic signal, the sum is discrete:

    $$ \omega_2 \;=\; 2\cdot\omega $$ $$ \omega_3 \;=\; 3\cdot\omega $$ $$ \dots $$ $$ \omega_n \;=\; n\cdot\omega $$

    where $ \omega $ is the fundamental frequency,
    and the subscript indexes enumerate harmonics (not overtones);
    i.e. 1 for fundamental, 0 for contingent non-oscillating term;
    then 2 for overtone 1, &c.

    and can therefore be effectively expressed as a literal sum:

    $$ f(t) \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^\infty \alpha[n]\cos\bigl(n\omega t + \phi[n]\bigr) $$

    Notice that $ \omega $ is a constant.

    $ \alpha[n] \; $ means that each partial has its own amplitude without necessarily being mathematically related to $ n $.
    (It's an index, not necessarily a function — although it could be. It could also be $ \, = \, 0 \; $.)

    Same goes for $ \; \phi[n] \; $.
    The partials don't have to be in phase for the signal to be periodic.

    I thought so. I checked, I was wrong.

    If the partials are not in phase, the period will have a random shape but it will be repeated at the fundamental frequency.

    Which is pretty obvious once you start thinking about it...
    but first you have to start thinking about it.

    Digression: what happens when $ \; n \, = \, 0 \; $?

    DC offset

    In an analogue circuit, the fact that $ \; \alpha\cos(\omega t + \phi) \; $ changes sign each half-period corresponds to the current changing direction, as is normal with AC (alternating current).

    A direct current (DC) can be either positive or negative but its sign (polarity) doesn't change.

    When $ \; n \, = \, 0 \; $:

    $$ \alpha[n]\cos\bigl( n\omega t + \phi[n] \bigr) \;=\; \alpha[0]\cos\bigl( \phi[0] \bigr) $$

    Which is a constant.

    It's called a DC offset.

    DC because it doesn't alternate.
    Offset because if you plot $ \; f(t) \; $ it will shift the curve of the whole signal up or down by its absolute value.

    In this regard it could be extracted from the sum:

    $$ f(t) \;=\; \alpha[0] \,+\, \sum_{n=1}^\infty \alpha[n]\cos\bigl( n\omega t + \phi[n] \bigr) $$

    Why $ \; \cos( \phi[0] ) \; $ disappeared gets answered right away.

    I find this concept quite intuitive: the frequency is the speed of the oscillations.

    When the speed is zero, there are no oscillations.
    If there are no oscillations, there is no period.

    If there is no period there is no phase.

    A mathematical proof could be: $ \: \omega[n] \, = \, n\omega $
    then:

    $$ f[n](t) \;=\; \alpha[n]\cos\bigl( \omega[n]t + \phi[n] \bigr) $$

    (equation [5] $$ f(t) \;=\; \alpha\cos(\omega t + \phi) $$ )

    $$ \begin{alignedat}{4} \text{Then since:}\quad && \phi[n] &\;=\;&& \omega[n]\,\cdot\,-t_0[n] \\[0.2em] \text{if:}\quad && \omega[n] &\;=\;&& 0 \\[0.2em] \text{then:}\quad && \phi[n] &\;=\;&& 0 \\[0.2em] \text{and since:}\quad && \omega[n] &\;=\;&& 0 \quad\text{when}\quad n \,=\, 0 \\[0.2em] \text{then:}\quad && \phi[0] &\;=\;&& 0 \\[0.2em] \text{therefore:}\quad && \cos\bigl( \phi[0] \bigr) &\;=\;&& \cos(0) \;=\; 1 \end{alignedat} $$

    Then:

    $$ f[0](t) \;=\; \alpha[0] $$

    (The actual DC offset.)

    In practice, a pure DC signal, when played on a monitoring system, has the membrane stuck in a non-zero fixed position.
    This can kill the monitoring system.

    My personal advice is to use visual tools and disable monitoring while testing DSP code (first at least).

    One last thing:

    the angular frequency $ \; \omega \, = \, 2\pi\f \; $ is essentially a mathematical tool used to embed the natural period of trigonometric functions together with the Hertzian frequency in a single factor, a way to shorten expressions.

    $ 2\pi\f \, = \, 2\pi/T \; $ is a ratio: a full circle compared to the period.

    When time is an integer multiple of $ T $ ($ \; t \, = \, nT \; $), this ratio denotes an integer number of circles —
    — the end of a cycle ($ \; 2\pi\f t \, = \, 2\pi t/T \, = \, 2\pi nT/T \, = \, 2n\pi \; $).

    So Hertz ($ \, = \, $ seconds$ {}^{-1} $) are indeed cycles per second, and, therefore, the practically useful datum.

    Then the variable with respect to which we integrate is actually $ \f $.

    Then:

    $$ f(t) \;=\; \int\limits_0^\infty \alpha[\omega]\cos\bigl( \omega t + \phi[\omega] \bigr)d\f $$

    This new accumulated sum is:

    $$ \Sigma_{w,n}(x) \;=\; \sum_{\nu=0}^{n-1} xw^\nu \;=\; \sum_{\nu=1}^n xw^{\nu-1} \;=\; S_{w,n}(x) \, / \, w $$ $$ \iff\quad S_{w,n}(x) \;=\; w\cdot\Sigma_{w,n}(x) $$

    Now if we subtract $ \, S_{w,n}(x) \, $ from $ \, \Sigma_{w,n}(x) \, $, we get:

    $$ \Sigma_{w,n}(x) \,-\, S_{w,n}(x) \;=\; \Sigma_{w,n}(x) \,-\, w\cdot\Sigma_{w,n}(x) $$ $$ \;=\; (1 - w)\cdot\Sigma_{w,n}(x) $$ $$ =\; \sum_{\nu=0}^{n-1} xw^\nu -\, \sum_{\nu=1}^n xw^\nu =\; xw^0 - xw^n =\; x(1 - w^n) $$

    or, in case you prefer the ellipsis notation:

    $$ =\; \left\{\; \begin{alignedat}{13} & x &&\,+\,&& xw &&\,+\,&& xw^2 &&\,+\,&& \dots &&\,+\,&& xw^{n-2} &&\,+\,&& xw^{n-1} \\ & &&\,-\,&& xw &&\,-\,&& xw^2 &&\,-\,&& \dots &&\,-\,&& xw^{n-2} &&\,-\,&& xw^{n-1} &&\,-\,&& xw^n \end{alignedat} \right. $$ $$ =\; x - xw^n \;=\; x(1 - w^n) $$

    (Same result.)

    So, assuming $ \; w \,\neq\, 1 \; $:

    $$ (1 - w)\cdot\Sigma_{w,n}(x) \;=\; x(1 - w^n) $$ $$ \iff\quad \Sigma_{w,n}(x) \;=\; x\frac{1-w^n}{1-w} $$
