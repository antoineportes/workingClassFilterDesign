    A polynomial is an exhaustive set of equivalent polynomial expressions.
    A polynomial expression is an arithmetical expression mixing constants and variables.

    An arithmetical expression is a mathematical expression involving only addition and multiplication (sums and products).

    An addition is sometimes called a subtraction (difference) — usually when involving one or several negative numbers.

    A multiplication is sometimes called a division (ratio) — usually when involving one or several rational numbers.

    The notation of a repeated multiplication of the same term by itself can be simplified with exponentiation of said term to a (non-strictly) positive integer power.
    (Assuming any product is quietly multiplied by one;
    and when multiplying a number zero time by itself, this previously implicit one is all that remains.)

    Because of this property, classical definitions often present exponentiation as a third type of allowed operation.

    This is valid for polynomials as long as the exponents are positive integers.
    That is: a polynomial can feature exponentiation if and only if said exponentiation is in fact a product.

    Choose the less confusing formulation of the definition.
    I like mine better (hopefully), but perhaps I'm weird.

    A polynomial can be defined by several distinct (but equal) expressions.
    One of them is called canonical (or standard).

    It's obtained by making use of commutativity, associativity and distributivity so that each instance of a variable (or product of variables if more than one) with the same exponent(s) appears only once, therefore having a single constant factor.

    E.g.:

    $$ ax^2 \,+\, bx^2 \;=\; (a \,+\, b)x^2 $$

    This single constant factor is called a coefficient.

    A coefficient's association with its corresponding exponentiation of the variable forms a term.

    The canonical form is the sum of all terms, sorted left-right from highest to lowest exponent.

    Each term has a degree, equal to its exponent (or sum of exponents if multivariable).

    The degree of the polynomial is the highest degree in the canonical form.

    The term of highest degree is called the leading term. Its coefficient is called the leading coefficient.

    Any coefficient can be zero except the leading coefficient.

    If the leading coefficient is 1, the polynomial is called a monic polynomial.

    The coefficient associated with the zeroth power of the variable is called the constant term.

    Note that, since there's a zeroth term, the number of terms is greater than the degree by 1 — if we include potential null terms.

    E.g. (1):
    $$ x^4 \,+\, 2 \;=\; (1) x^4 \,+\, (0) x^3 \,+\, (0) x^2 \,+\, (0) x^1 \,+\, (2) x^0 $$
    degree:
    4
    terms:
    5
    E.g. (2):

    $ y \,=\, x \; $ is a special case of the affine function: $ \; y \;=\; {a_1}x^1 \:+\: {a_0}x^0 $
    which is a polynomial of degree 1 with coefficients $ \; a_1 \,=\, 1 \; $ and $ \; a_0 \,=\, 0 \; $.

    In this case, $ a_1 $ is the leading coefficient, $ \; a_1 \, x \; $ is the leading term, $ a_0 $ is the constant term.

    E.g. (3):

    $ y \,=\, 1.33 \; $ is equivalent to $ \; y \;=\; 1.33 \, x^0 $
    which is a polynomial of degree 0 whose unique coefficient is $ \; a_0 \, = \, 1.33 \; $.

    In this case, the leading coefficient, the leading term, and the constant term are indistinguishably: $ a_0 $.

    E.g. (4):

    $ y \,=\, 0 \; $ is a problem.

    And of course: $ \; y \;=\; {a_2}x^2 \,+\, {a_1}x \,+\, a_0 \; $ is a polynomial of degree 2 — as long as $ a_2 $ is non-zero.

    The canonical form:

    $$ {a_N}x^N \,+\, {a_{N-1}}x^{N-1} \,+\, \dots \,+\, {a_1}x \,+\, a_0 $$

    or, equivalently:

    $$ \sum_{n=0}^N{a_n}x^n $$

    can be re-expressed as:

    $$ {a_N} \Bigl( x^N \,+\, \bigl( a_{N-1}/a_N \bigr) x^{N-1} \,+\, \dots \,+\, \bigl( a_1/a_N \bigr) x \,+\, a_0/a_N \Bigr) $$

    or, better yet:

    $$ a_N\sum_{n=0}^N \frac{a_n}{a_N} \, x^n $$

    This factorisation allows to extract a monic polynomial out of any polynomial.
    (And it's valid because: $ a_N $ is non-zero by definition of a polynomial.)

    Beware of amphibology:
    this expression is sometimes loosely referred to as factored form — but this name is more typically reserved for a distinct form, with which we'll deal in a few lines.

    I won't be using this equivocal acception, but know that it happens elsewhere.

    Factoring out the leading coefficient to extract a monic is quite often called normalising the polynomial.
    Therefore, should we need a name for this form, let's agree on normalised form.

    A polynomial with a single term is called a monomial. Two terms? Binomial. Three terms? Trinomial. &c.

    (E.g.: $ \; x^7 \; $ is a septic monic monomial...)

    A polynomial with strictly real coefficients in canonical form is called a real polynomial.
    (If the canonical form has only real coefficients, so does the normalised form.)

    If it has complex coefficients, it's a complex polynomial.
    (Real numbers are special cases of complex numbers.
    Real polynomials are special cases of complex polynomials.)

    If it's not in canonical (or normalised) form, we cannot say whether it's real.

    Important distinction: real in real polynomial only concerns the coefficients — the variable can still be complex.

    A polynomial of negative degree is called a Laurent polynomial (named after Pierre Alphonse Laurent).

    More rigorously:

    a Laurent polynomial can have a positive degree;
    its specificity is that it allows negative exponents.

    Since $ \; x^{-n} \,=\, 1/x^n \; $, a Laurent polynomial is not defined at $ \; x \,=\, 0 \; $.
    (We won't be needing Laurent polynomials before Chapter vi.)

    We now see how whether division happens in polynomials really depends on perspective: textbooks tend to answer no, never.

    Let's be more rigorous than textbooks:
    the canonical and normalised forms of non-Laurent polynomials are not supposed to show division...

    But if I write:

    $$ f(x) \;=\; \frac{x/11 \,-\, 17/x}{15/10} \;=\; \frac{1}{11\cdot3/2} \, x \;+\; \frac{-17}{3/2} \, x^{-1} $$ $$ \hphantom{ f(x) } \;=\; (2/33)x^1 \,+\, 0x^0 \,+\, (-34/3)x^{-1} $$

    $ f(x) \; $ is a Laurent polynomial of the first degree.

    Its leading coefficient is: $ \; 2/33 \; $.
    Its constant term is: $ \; 0 \; $.

    Fun fact:
    $ a_0 $ is the only coefficient not multiplied by a non-zero power of $ x $.

    So no matter the (non-Laurent) polynomial's degree, its value at $ \; x \, = \, 0 \; $ is always $ a_0 $.

    (Fun is open for debate, but a fact it most certainly is.)

    The values of the variable(s) at which a polynomial outputs zero are called its  r o o t s .

    $ f(x) \; $ is a polynomial? $ \; f(r) \,=\, 0 \; $? Then $ r $ is a root of $ \; f(x) \; $.
    $ f(x) \; $ is a polynomial? $ \; f(x) \,=\, 0 \; $ has 17 solutions for $ x $? $ \; f(x) \; $ has 17 roots.

    (Sometimes, roots are called zeros or zeroes — in the context of filter design, this would be confusing. I'll stick to roots.)

    Let's build stuff.

    Degree 0 constant

    $$ f(x) \;=\; a_0 $$

    Then $ \; f(x) \,=\, 0 \; $ means $ \; a_0 \,=\, 0 \; $ which has 1 solution for $ a_0 $ but none for $ x $.
    Therefore a polynomial of the zeroth degree has zero root.

    Degree 1 affine or linear

    $$ f(x) \;=\; a_1 x \,+\, a_0 $$ $$ f(x) \,=\, 0 \quad\iff\quad a_1 x \,+\, a_0 \;=\; 0 \quad\iff\quad a_1 (x \,+\, a_0/a_1) \;=\; 0 $$ $$ \hphantom{ f(x) \,=\, 0 } \quad\iff\quad x \;=\; -a_0/a_1 $$

    This normalising factorisation is relevant because $ a_1 $ is non-zero by definition of a polynomial.

    Therefore a polynomial of the first degree has one root: $ \; r_1 \,=\, -a_0/a_1 \; $.

    And:

    $$ a_1 (x \,+\, a_0/a_1) \;=\; {a_1}(x - r_1) $$

    shows that it can be expressed in terms of this root.

    And this is the factored form.

    This is very important, so just to make sure you don't rush over this tiny line:
    The factored form is the expression of a polynomial in terms of its roots.

    Degree 2 quadratic

    $$ a_2 x^2 \,+\, a_1 x \,+\, a_0 \;=\; 0 \quad\iff\quad a_2 \bigl( x^2 \,+\, (a_1/a_2)x \,+\, a_0/a_2 \bigr) \;=\; 0 $$

    Let's consider the general case:

    $$ (\alpha + \beta)^2 \;=\; (\alpha + \beta)(\alpha + \beta) \;=\; \alpha\alpha \,+\, \alpha\beta \,+\, \beta\alpha\,+\, \beta\beta \;=\; \alpha^2 \,+\, 2\alpha\beta \,+\, \beta^2 $$

    Now $ \; x^2 \,+\, (a_1/a_2)x \; $ has the form: $ \; \alpha^2 \,+\, 2\alpha\beta \; $ where:

    $$ \alpha \;=\; x $$

    and

    $$ a_1/a_2 \;=\; 2\beta $$

    so

    $$ \beta \;=\; a_1/(2a_2) $$

    Then:

    $$ \beta^2 \;=\; {a_1}^2/(2a_2)^2 $$

    We can add any term we want to the equation, as long as it happens on both sides, so:

    $$ x^2 \,+\, (a_1/a_2)x \,+\, {a_1}^2/(2a_2)^2 \;=\; {a_1}^2/(2a_2)^2 \,-\, a_0/a_2 $$ $$ \iff\quad \bigl( x \,+\, a_1/(2a_2) \bigr)^2 \;=\; {a_1}^2/(2a_2)^2 \,-\, a_0/a_2 $$ $$ \hphantom{ \iff\quad \bigl( x \,+\, a_1/(2a_2) \bigr)^2 } \;=\; \frac{ {a_1}^2 \,-\, 2^2 a_2 a_0 }{ (2a_2)^2 } $$ $$ \iff\quad x \,+\, a_1/(2a_2) \;=\; \pm\sqrt{ \frac{ {a_1}^2 \,-\, 4 a_2 a_0 }{ (2a_2)^2 } } $$ $$ \hphantom{ \iff\quad x \,+\, a_1/(2a_2) } \;=\; \pm\frac{ \sqrt{ {a_1}^2 \,-\, 4 a_2 a_0 } }{ 2 a_2 } $$ $$ \iff\quad x \;=\; \pm\frac{ \sqrt{ {a_1}^2 \,-\, 4 a_2 a_0 } }{ 2a_2 } \:-\: \frac{a_1}{2 a_2} $$ $$ \hphantom{ \iff\quad x } \;=\; \frac{ -a_1 \,\pm\, \sqrt{ {a_1}^2 \,-\, 4 a_2 a_0 } }{ 2 a_2 } $$

    $ {a_1}^2 \,-\, 4 a_2 a_0 \; $ is called the polynomial's discriminant.
    It's usually notated $ \Delta $ (upper—case delta).

    $$ ax \,+\, b $$

    $$ ax^2 \,+\, bx \,+\, c $$

    is the product of two first-degree polynomials,
    whose respective roots are also the roots of said second-degree polynomial.

    What if... $ \; x \,=\, z \; $?

    $$ P_N(z) \;=\; (z - z)Q_{N-1}(z) \,+\, R $$ $$ \iff\quad R \;=\; P_N(z) $$

    This is the polynomial remainder theorem.

    It basically says that instead of bothering with polynomial long division,
    when the divisor is of degree 1,
    you can get the remainder
    by using the opposite of the constant term of the divisor's normalised form
    (i.e. its root) as the dividend's variable.

    The divisor must be normalised by factoring out its leading coefficient:

    $$ ax \,+\, b \quad\longleftarrow\quad a \left(\, x \,+\, b/a \,\right) $$

    then:

    $$ \frac{ P_N(x) \,-\, R }{ a( x + b/a ) } \;=\; Q_{N-1}(x) $$ $$ \iff\quad P_N(x) \;=\; a( a + b/a )Q_{N-1}(x) \,+\, R $$ $$ \implies\quad P_N(-b/a) \;=\; a(-b/a \,+\, b/a)Q_{N-1}(-b/a) \,+\, R $$ $$ \hphantom{ \implies\quad P_N(-b/a) } \;=\; R $$

    $ P_N(x) $
    $ \quad\longleftarrow\quad P_N(x)/a $
    $ R $
    $ \quad\longleftarrow\quad R(x)/a $

    What this proves:
    when we divide a non-constant polynomial by a monic binomial of the first degree whose constant term is the opposite of any of the roots of the dividend —
    — there is no remainder.

    The monic and the quotient are called factors of the dividend polynomial.

    This is the reason for the factored form's name.

    Plain English may not offer the clearest formulation:

    $$ \left. \begin{alignedat}{1} & P_a(x) \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^N a_n x^n \\[0.25em] & P_a(r_1) \;=\; 0 \end{alignedat} \;\right\} \;\iff\quad P_a(x) \;=\; (x - r_1) \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} b_n x^n $$

    Let's define:

    $$ P_b(x) \;=\; P_a(x)/(x - r_1) \;=\; \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} b_n x^n $$

    and:

    $$ P_b(r_2) \;=\; 0 $$

    then:

    $$ P_b(x) \;=\; (x - r_2) \sum_{n=0}^{N-2} c_n x^n $$ $$ \implies\quad P_a(x) \;=\; (x - r_1)(x - r_2) \sum_{n=0}^{N-2} c_n x^n $$ $$ P_a(r_2) \;=\; 0 $$

    So any root of $ \; P_b(x) \; $ is also a root of $ \; P_a(x) \; $.

    And, of course, $ r_1 $ is the root of $ \; x - r_1 \; $,
    and $ r_2 $ is the root of $ \; x - r_2 \; $.

    What this proves:
    all roots of a polynomial's factor are roots of said polynomial.

    This is the factor theorem.

    If we keep running this loop,
    knowing that the degree of the quotient is the degree of the dividend minus the degree of the divisor,
    and that the degree of our monic first-degree binomial divisor is always 1,
    we'll end up with $ \; P_{???}(x) \; $ being a constant factor — let's call it $ Q $.

    $$ P_a(x) \;=\; Q \prod_{\nu=1}^N (x - r_\nu) $$

    What this proves:

    the degree of a polynomial is equal to the number of its roots.

    Equalities read in both directions.

    So:

    1. if a polynomial has $ N $ roots, it's of degree $ N $;
    2. if it's of degree $ N $, it has $ N $ roots.

    $ Q $ being constant, the only solutions to $ \; P_a(x) \,=\, 0 \; $ are $ \; x \,=\, r_\nu \; $.

    It's an exact number if we admit duplicates as distinct.
    (Distinct duplicates is very oxymoronic, but it's the best way I could find to characterise every possible polynomial in a general statement.)

    Here, 11 has a multiplicity of 3.

    $$ \sum_{n=1}^N a_n x^n \;=\; a_N \prod_{\nu=1}^N (x - r_\nu) $$

    This is the true power of the factor theorem.

    And it's our if and only if.

    Meaning:

    any product of $ N $ 1st-degree polynomials
    is an $ N $th-degree polynomial;

    and:

    any $ N $th-degree polynomial

    Another way to phrase this conclusion is:
    a polynomial and its non-constant factors have the same roots;
    and the degree of a polynomial is the number of its non-constant factors.
