    Polynomials: game rules

    A polynomial is an exhaustive set of equivalent polynomial expressions.
    A polynomial expression is an arithmetical expression mixing constants and variables.
    An arithmetical expression is a mathematical expression involving only addition and multiplication (sums and products).
    An addition is sometimes called a subtraction (difference) — usually when involving one or several negative numbers.
    A multiplication is sometimes called a division (ratio) — usually when involving one or several rational numbers.
    The notation of a repeated multiplication of the same term by itself can be simplified with exponentiation of said term to a positive integer power.

    Because of this property, classical definitions often present exponentiation as a third type of allowed operation.
    This is valid for polynomials as long as the exponents are positive integers.
    That is: a polynomial can feature exponentiation if and only if said exponentiation is in fact a product.

    Choose the less confusing formulation of the definition.
    I like mine better (hopefully), but perhaps I'm weird.

    A polynomial can be defined by several distinct (but equal) expressions.
    One of them is called canonical (or standard).
    It's obtained by making use of commutativity, associativity and distributivity so that each instance of a variable (or product of variables if more than one) with the same exponent(s) appears only once, therefore having a single constant factor.

    E.g.: $ \quad ax^2 \, + \, bx^2 \; = \; (a \, + \, b)x^2 $

    This single constant factor is called a coefficient.
    A coefficient's association with its corresponding exponentiation of the variable forms a term.
    The canonical form is the sum of all terms.
    Each term has a degree, equal to its exponent.
    The degree, or order, of the polynomial is the highest degree in the canonical form.
    The term of highest degree is called the leading term. Its coefficient is called the leading coefficient.
    Any coefficient can be zero except the leading coefficient.
    If the leading coefficient is 1, the polynomial is called a monic polynomial.
    The coefficient associated with the zeroth power of the variable is called the constant term.

    Note that, since there's a zeroth term, the number of terms is greater than the degree / oder by 1 — if we include potential null terms.

    E.g. (1):
    $$ x^4 \, + \, 2 \; = \; (1) x^4 \, + \, (0) x^3 \, + \, (0) x^2 \, + \, (0) x^1 \, + \, (2) x^0 $$ $$ \begin{align} \text{degree:}& \quad 4 \\ \text{terms:}& \quad 5 \end{align} $$
    E.g. (2):

    $ y \, = \, x \; $ is a special case of the affine function: $ \; y \; = \; {a_1}x^1 \: + \: {a_0}x^0 $
    which is a polynomial of order 1 with coefficients $ \; a_1 \, = \, 1 \; $ and $ \; a_0 \, = \, 0 \; $.
    In this case, $ a_1 $ is the leading coefficient, $ \; a_1 \, x \; $ is the leading term, $ a_0 $ is the constant term.

    E.g. (3):

    $ y \, = \, 1.33 \; $ is equivalent to $ \; y \; = \; 1.33 \, x^0 $
    which is a polynomial of order 0 whose unique coefficient is $ \; a_0 \, = \, 1.33 \; $.
    In this case, the leading coefficient, the leading term, and the constant term are indistinguishably: $ a_0 $.

    And of course: $ \quad y \; = \; {a_2}x^2 \: + \: {a_1}x \: + \: a_0 \quad $ is a polynomial of order 2 — as long as $ a_2 $ is non-zero.

    The canonical form:

    $$ {a_N}x^N \: + \: {a_{N-1}}x^{(N-1)} \: + \: \dots \: + \: {a_1}x \: + \: a_0 $$

    or, equivalently:

    $$ \sum_{n=0}^{N}{a_n}x^n $$

    can be re-expressed as:

    $$ {a_N} \Bigl( x^N \: + \: \bigl( {a_{N-1}}/a_N \bigr)x^{(N-1)} \: + \: \dots \: + \: \bigl( {a_1} / {a_N} \bigr)x \: + \: {a_0}/{a_N} \Bigr) $$

    or, better yet:

    $$ {a_N}\sum_{n=0}^{N} \dfrac{a_n}{a_N} \, x^n $$

    This factorisation allows to extract a monic polynomial out of any polynomial.
    (And it's valid because: $ a_N $ is non-zero by definition of a polynomial.)

    Beware of amphibology: this expression is sometimes loosely referred to as factored form — but this name is more typically reserved for a distinct form, with which we'll deal in a few lines.

    I won't be using this equivocal acception, but know that it happens elsewhere.

    Factoring out the leading coefficient to extract a monic is quite often called normalising the polynomial.
    Therefore, should we need a name for this form, let's agree on normalised form.

    A polynomial with a single term is called a monomial. Two terms? Binomial. Three terms? Trinomial. Etc.

    (E.g.: $ \; x^7 \; $ is a septic monic monomial...)

    A polynomial with strictly real coefficients in canonical form is called a real polynomial.
    (If the canonical form has only real coefficients, so does the normalised form.)

    If it has complex coefficients, it's a complex polynomial.
    (Real numbers are special cases of complex numbers. Real polynomials are special cases of complex polynomials.)

    If it's not in canonical (or normalised) form, we cannot say whether it's real.

    Important distinction: real in real polynomial only concerns the coefficients — the variable can still be complex.

    A polynomial of negative order is called a Laurent polynomial (named after Pierre Alphonse Laurent).

    More rigorously:

    a Laurent polynomial can have a positive order;
    its specificity is that it allows negative exponents.

    Since $ \; x^{-n} \, = \, 1/x^n \; $, a Laurent polynomial is not defined at $ \; x \, = \, 0 \; $.
    (We won't be needing Laurent polynomials before Chapter vii.)

    We now see how whether division happens in polynomials really depends on perspective: textbooks tend to answer no, never.

    Let's be more rigorous than textbooks:
    the canonical and normalised forms of non-Laurent polynomials are not supposed to show division...

    But if I write:

    $$ f(x) \; = \; \frac{x/11 \, - \, 17/x}{15/10} \; = \; \frac{1}{11 \cdot 3/2} \, x \; + \; \frac{-17}{3/2} \, x^{-1} $$ $$ \hphantom{ f(x) \; } = \; (2/33)x^1 \, + \, 0x^0 \, + \, (-34/3)x^{-1} $$

    $ f(x) \; $ is a Laurent polynomial of the first order.

    Its leading coefficient is: $ \; 2/33 \; $.
    Its constant term is: $ \; 0 \; $.

    Fun fact: $ a_0 $ is the only coefficient not multiplied by a non-zero power of $ x $.

    So no matter the (non-Laurent) polynomial's order, its value at $ \; x \, = \, 0 \; $ is always $ a_0 $.

    (Fun is open for debate, but a fact it most certainly is.)

    The values of the variable(s) at which a polynomial outputs zero are called its  r o o t s .

    $ f(x) \; $ is a polynomial? $ \; f(r) \, = \, 0 \; $? Then $ r $ is a root of $ \; f(x) \; $.
    $ f(x) \; $ is a polynomial? $ \; f(x) \, = \, 0 \; $ has 17 solutions for $ x $? $ \; f(x) \; $ has 17 roots.

    (Sometimes, roots are called zeros or zeroes — in the context of filter design, this would be confusing. I'll stick to roots.)

    Let's build stuff.

    Order 0 constant

    $$ f(x) \; = \; a_0 $$

    Then $ \; f(x) \, = \, 0 \; $ means $ \; a_0 \, = \, 0 \; $ which has 1 solution for $ a_0 $ but none for $ x $.
    Therefore a polynomial of the zeroth order has zero root.

    Order 1 affine or linear

    $$ f(x) \; = \; {a_1}x \, + \, a_0 $$ $$ f(x) \, = \, 0 \quad\iff\quad {a_1}x \, + \, a_0 \; = \; 0 \quad\iff\quad {a_1}(x \, + \, {a_0}/{a_1}) \; = \; 0 $$ $$ \hphantom{f(x) \, = \, 0 \quad} \iff\quad x \; = \; {-a_0}/{a_1} $$

    This normalising factorisation is relevant because $ a_1 $ is non-zero by definition of a polynomial.

    Therefore a polynomial of the first order has one root: $ \; r_1 \, = \, -a_0/a_1 \; $.

    And:

    $$ a_1 \, (x \, + \, a_0/a_1) \; = \; {a_1}(x - r_1) $$

    shows that it can be expressed in terms of this root.

    And this is the factored form.

    This is very important, so just to make sure you don't rush over this tiny line:
    The factored form is the expression of a polynomial in terms of its roots.

    Order 2 quadratic

    $$ {a_2}x^2 \, + \, {a_1}x \, + \, a_0 \; = \; 0 \quad\iff\quad a_2 \, \bigl( x^2 \, + \, (a_1/a_2)x \, + \, a_0/a_2 \bigr) \; = \; 0 $$

    [blah] $ a_2 $ [blah] non-zero [blah] definition &c.

    $$ \iff\quad x^2 \, + \, (a_1/a_2)x \, + \, a_0/a_2 \; = \; 0 \quad\iff\quad x^2 \, + \, (a_1/a_2)x \; = \; -a_0/a_2 $$

    Pause. Let's consider the general case:

    $$ (\alpha + \beta)^2 \; = \; (\alpha + \beta)(\alpha + \beta) \; = \; \alpha\alpha \, + \, \alpha\beta \, + \, \beta\alpha \, + \, \beta\beta \; = \; \alpha^2 \, + \, 2\alpha\beta \, + \, \beta^2 $$

    Now $ \; x^2 \, + \, (a_1/a_2)x \; $ has the form: $ \; \alpha^2 \, + \, 2\alpha\beta \; $ where:

    $$ \alpha \, = \, x $$

    and

    $$ a_1/a_2 \, = \, 2\beta $$

    so

    $$ \beta \; = \; a_1 / (2a_2) $$

    Then:

    $$ \beta^2 \; = \; {a_1}^2 / (2a_2)^2 $$

    We can add any term we want to the equation, as long as it happens on both sides, so:

    $$ x^2 \, + \, (a_1/a_2) x \, + \, {a_1}^2 / (2a_2)^2 \; = \; {a_1}^2 / (2a_2)^2 \, - \, {a_0}/{a_2} $$ $$ \iff\quad \bigl( x \, + \, a_1 / (2a_2) \bigr)^2 \; = \; {a_1}^2 / (2a_2)^2 \, - \, {a_0}/{a_2} \; = \; \frac{ {a_1}^2 \, - \, {2^2}{a_2}{a_0} }{ (2a_2)^2 } $$ $$ \iff\quad x \, + \, a_1 / (2a_2) \; = \; \pm \sqrt{ \frac{{a_1}^2 \, - \, 4{a_2}{a_0} }{ (2a_2)^2 } } \; = \; \pm \frac{ \sqrt{ {a_1}^2 \, - \, 4{a_2}{a_0} } }{ 2{a_2} } $$ $$ \iff\quad x \; = \; \pm \frac{ \sqrt{ {a_1}^2 \, - \, 4{a_2}{a_0} } }{ 2a_2 } \: - \: \frac{a_1}{2 \, a_2} \; = \; \frac{ - a_1 \, \pm \, \sqrt{ {a_1}^2 \, - \, 4{a_2}{a_0} } }{ 2a_2 } $$

    $ {a_1}^2 \, - \, 4{a_2}{a_0} \; $ is called the polynomial's discriminant. (Hence discriminating meaning finding the roots.)
    It's usually notated $ \Delta $ (upper—case delta).

    My notation is not standard (well it both is and is not, but you'll see that I'm not the inconsistent one here).

    Expressing polynomials' coefficients with indexes of $ a $ or $ b $ is standard (the latter usually being reserved for numators).

    But when dealing with the special cases of affine or quadratic, the classical notation is (respectively):

    $$ ax \, + \, b $$

    and

    $$ ax^2 \, + \, bx \, + \, c $$

    I don't like this. You get $ \; a_1 \, = \, a \; $, then $ \; a_1 \, = \, b \; $, etc. It's confusing for no reason.

    Plus it complies poorly with generalisation: if you keep raising the order you'll get $ \; a_0 \, = \, d \; $ (as in $ \; df/dx \; $...), then $ \; a_0 \, = \, e \; $ (as in $ \; \exp(1) \; $...), then $ \; a_0 \, = \, f \; $ (as in $ \; f(x) \; $ or $ \; 2\pi\f \; $...); orders 8 and 9 will give $ i $ and $ j $ (as in $ \; \sqrt{-1} \; $...); order 23 gives $ \; a_0 \, = \, x \; $...

    Order 26 starts using Greek letters.
    At some point you'll run out of alphabets. You'll never run out of numbers.

    So pick one letter, give it indexes and stick with it. Or, as usual, go play with fire.

    Anyway, don't be surprised if you come across: $ \; \Delta \, = \, b^2 \, - \, 4ac \; $.

    Therefore a polynomial of the second order has two roots:

    $$ r_1 \; = \; \frac{ -a_1 \, + \, \sqrt{\Delta} }{ 2a_2 } $$

    and

    $$ r_2 \; = \; \frac{ -a_1 \, - \, \sqrt{\Delta} }{ 2a_2 } $$

    Before moving further on: we're still talking about filters — even though from a distance.

    We might play around with complex numbers on our notebooks and GitHub repositories, but an analogue voltage, or its digital counterpart, needs to remain purely real.

    So I'm assuming real polynomials, starting now.

    What comes next might apply to complex polynomials, but I haven't checked. And I don't want to write something false because of too general phrasing.

    In short: from now on: $ \quad \Im(a_2) \, = \, \Im(a_1) \, = \, \Im(a_0) \, = \, 0 $

    Notice that if $ \Delta $ is real and negative, $ \sqrt{\Delta} $ is imaginary.

    Then $ r_1 $ and $ r_2 $ are complex numbers and:

    $$ \Re(r_1) \; = \; $$
    $$ \Re(r_2) \; = \; -{a_1}/(2a_2) $$
    $$ \Im(r_1) \; = \; $$
    $$ \hphantom{-} \sqrt{\Delta} / (2a_2) $$
    $$ \Im(r_2) \; = \; $$
    $$ -\sqrt{\Delta} / (2a_2) \; = \; -\Im(r_1) $$

    Then $ r_1 $ and $ r_2 $ are complex conjugates.
    If $ \Delta $ is real and positive, $ \sqrt{\Delta} $ is real and so are $ r_1 $ and $ r_2 $.

    Now if $ \; x \: = \: r_1 \; $, 
    $ \; x \, - \, r_1 \: = \: 0 \; $,
    and if $ \; x \: = \: r_2 \; $, 
    $ \; x \, - \, r_2 \: = \: 0 \; $.

    Then the roots of $ \; (x \, - \, r_1)(x \, - \, r_2) \; $ are $ r_1 $ and $ r_2 $.

    Notice that:

    $$ a_2 (x \, - \, r_1)(x \, - \, r_2) \; =\; \sqrt{a_2}(x \, - \, r_1) \cdot \sqrt{a_2}(x \, - \, r_2) $$

    and that $ \; \sqrt{a_2}(x \, - \, r_1) \; $ and $ \; \sqrt{a_2}(x \, - \, r_2) \; $ are two first—order polynomials whose respective roots are $ r_1 $ and $ r_2 $.

    Therefore any second-order polynomial
    is the product of two first-order polynomials,
    whose respective roots are also the roots of said second-order polynomial.

    If the second-order polynomial is real,
    the first-order ones are either both real or forming a conjugate pair.

    Fun example:

    $$ x^2 \, - \, 1 \; = \; 0 \quad\iff\quad x^2 \: = \: 1 $$ $$ \iff\quad x \; = \; \pm\sqrt{\, 1 \,} \; = \; \pm 1 $$

    So:

    $$ x^2 \, - \ 1 \; = \; (x \, + \, 1)(x \, - \, 1) $$

    $$ q \; = \; \floor(\delta/d) $$ $$ r \; = \; \delta \mod d \; = \; \delta \, - \, q \cdot d $$ $$ d \; \neq \; 0 $$
