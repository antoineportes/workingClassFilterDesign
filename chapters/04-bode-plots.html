    Working-class modulus fix ®

    Third verse, same as the first.

    This section will feel disproportionally long, but it's an opportunity to properly introduce functions that tend to end up quite ubiquitous in computer programming.

    Let's start with $ \min $ and $ \max $.

    These functions compare two real numbers and output one of them.

    $ \min $ outputs the smallest and $ \max $ outputs the biggest.

    If the numbers are equal, then they both output the common equal value.

    So:

    $$ \min(a \mathrel{,} b) \;=\; \left\{\: \begin{alignedat}{2} a & \;\quad\text{if}\quad a \,\leq\, b \\ b & \;\quad\text{if}\quad a \,\geq\, b \end{alignedat} \right. $$

    and:

    $$ \max(a \mathrel{,} b) \;=\; \left\{\: \begin{alignedat}{2} a & \;\quad\text{if}\quad a \,\geq\, b \\ b & \;\quad\text{if}\quad a \,\leq\, b \end{alignedat} \right. $$

    Or:

    max(a , b) = (a > b) * a + (a < b) * b + (a == b) * a ; min(a , b) = (a < b) * a + (a > b) * b + (a == b) * a ;

    or even:

    max(a , b) = (a >= b) * a + (a < b) * b ; min(a , b) = (a <= b) * a + (a > b) * b ;

    Then there's the signum function.

    It takes one complex variable and divides it by its absolute value.

    If the variable is zero, various implementations exist;
    but I strongly recommend the ones that output zero.

    Why? Empirical discrimination.

    If there's a use for other implementations, I've never met it.

    Desmos uses this implementation.

    Take the exponential form: $ \; z \,=\, \left| z \right| \exp\bigl( j\arg(z) \bigr) \; $,
    you get:

    $$ \sgn(z) \;=\; \left\{\: \begin{alignedat}{2} \exp\bigl( j\arg(z) \bigr) & \;\quad\text{if}\quad z \,\neq\, 0 \\ 0 & \;\quad\text{if}\quad z \,=\, 0 \end{alignedat} \right. $$

    Important corollary:

    $$ \sgn(z) \;=\; \left\{\: \begin{alignedat}{2} 0 & \;\quad\text{if}\quad z \,=\, 0 \\ z/\left| z \right| & \;\quad\text{if not} \end{alignedat} \right. $$ $$ \implies\quad z \;=\; \left| z \right| \sgn(z) \quad\forall\; z $$

    Which suspiciously resemble the complex exponential form.

    In fact:

    $$ \sgn(z) \;=\; \exp\bigl( j\arg(z) \bigr) $$ $$ \implies\quad \arg(z) \;=\; \frac{ \ln\bigl( \sgn(z) \bigr) }{j} $$

    which is undefined at zero, as it should be.

    Notice that it's undefined whether $ \; \sgn(0) \,=\, 0 \; $ or $ \; \sgn(0) \,=\, $ NaN .

    Is zero positive or negative?
    Either neither or both.

    If $ z $ is real and positive, $ \; \arg(z) \,=\, 0 \; $,
    and:

    $$ \exp\bigl( j\arg(z) \bigr) \;=\; \cos(0) \,+\, j\sin(0) \;=\; 1 \,+\, j\cdot0 \;=\; 1 $$

    (or simply: $ \; \exp(0) \,=\, 1 \; $)

    If it's real and negative, $ \; \arg(z) \,=\, \pi \; $,
    and:

    $$ \exp\bigl( j\arg(z) \bigr) \;=\; \cos(\pi) \,+\, j\sin(\pi) \;=\; -1 \,+\, j\cdot0 \;=\; -1 $$

    So:

    $$ \sgn\bigl( \Re(z) \bigr) \;=\; \left\{ \begin{alignedat}{2} +1 & \;\quad\text{if}\quad \Re(z) \,\gt\, 0 \\ 0 & \;\quad\text{if}\quad \Re(z) \, = \, 0 \\ -1 & \;\quad\text{if}\quad \Re(z) \,\lt\, 0 \end{alignedat} \right. $$

    A C++ hack for signum with real arguments could be:

    template <typename T> int sgn(T x) { return ( x > T(0) ) - ( x < T(0) ) ; }
    $ x $ positive
    $ \!\longrightarrow\quad $ true$ {}\,-\,{} $false$ {}\;=\; 1 \,-\, 0 \;=\; +1 $
    $ x $ negative
    $ \!\longrightarrow\quad $false$ {}\,-\,{} $true $ {}\;=\; 0 \,-\, 1 \;=\; -1 $
    $ x $ null
    $ \!\longrightarrow\quad $false$ {}\,-\,{} $false$ {}\;=\; 0 \,-\, 0 \;=\; \hphantom{+} 0 $

    Now the truncating functions.

    I know four of them: $ \floor $, $ \ceil $, $ \trunc $ and $ \round $.

    Say $ x $ is a real number.

    In base 10, $ x $ can be considered the sum of its integer part and its decimal part.

    To include negative numbers, these parts must incorporate $ x $'s sign.

    E.g.:

    $$ x \;=\; 0.70710678 \;=\; 0 \,+\, .70710678 $$ $$ \implies\; \left\{\; \begin{alignedat}{2} \INT(x) & \:=\: 0 \\ \DEC(x) & \:=\: .70710678 \end{alignedat} \right. $$ $$ x \;=\; 0.61803399\dots \;=\; 0 \,+\, .61803399\dots $$ $$ \implies\; \left\{\; \begin{alignedat}{2} \INT(x) & \:=\: 1 \\ \DEC(x) & \:=\: .61803399\dots \end{alignedat} \right. $$ $$ x \;=\; -1000.0001 \;=\; -1000 \,-\, .0001 $$ $$ \implies\; \left\{\; \begin{alignedat}{2} \INT(x) & \:=\: -1000 \\ \DEC(x) & \:=\: -.0001 \end{alignedat} \right. $$

    $ \trunc(x) \; $ simply outputs $ \; \INT(x) \; $.

    $ \ceil(x) \; $ outputs the the smallest integer greater than or equal to $ x $;
    if $ x $ is negative, that's $ \; \INT(x) \; $, or $ \; \trunc(x) \; $;
    if it's positive, that's $ \; \INT(x) + 1 \; $ or $ \; \trunc(x) + 1 \; $.
    (Unless $ x $ is an integer: then the output is just $ x $.)

    $$ \ceil(x) \;=\; \INT(x) \,+\, \max\Bigl( \sgn\bigl( \DEC(x) \bigr) \mathrel{,} 0 \Bigr) $$

    $ \round(x) \; $ outputs the closest integer to $ x $.

    There is a debate over what to do with $ .5 $, on which I'll elaborate if I ever use the $ \round $ function.

    Finally, $ \; \floor(x) \; $ outputs the greatest integer less than or equal to $ x $;
    if $ x $ is positive, it's $ \; \INT(x) \; $, or $ \; \trunc(x) \; $;
    if it's negative, it's $ \; \INT(x) - 1 \; $, or $ \; \trunc(x) - 1 \; $.
    (Unless $ x $ is an integer: then the output is just $ x $.)

    $$ \floor(x) \;=\; \INT(x) \,+\, \min\Bigl( \sgn\bigl( \DEC(x) \bigr) \mathrel{,} 0 \Bigr) $$

    Classically, we say that:

    • $ \ceil $ truncates towards plus infinity;
    • $ \trunc $ truncates towards zero;
    • $ \floor $ truncates towards minus infinity.

    First time I've stumbled upon these descriptions, I've found them more confusing than helping.

    Then I've studied truncation intensively and realised how efficient and clever they are.

    From my (non-universal) standpoint: they have poor pedagogical value, but their completeness and conciseness makes them valuable mnemonics once you've clearly grasped the inherent mechanics of the three functions.

    Notation

    $ \floor(x) \; $ is also written $ \ \lfloor x \rfloor \; $.

    $ \ceil(x) \; $ is also written $ \ \lceil x \rceil \; $.

    $ \floor $ is almost the only one invoked in this book.

    Mainly because it's the only one of the four that systematically preserves the variable's sign.

    $$ \round(-0.49) \;=\; 0 $$ $$ \ceil(-0.999) \;=\; 0 $$ $$ \trunc(-0.999) \;=\; 0 $$ $$ \floor(-0.0001) \;=\; -1 $$

    It's inherently bilateral-friendly, and therefore the one required for an implementation of the modulo function, if we want negative moduli to comply with trigonometric periodicity.

    I've arrived at this conclusion while painfully crafting the eponymous Working-class modulus fix.

    Only to eventually find out that Donald Knuth had already done the hard work.
    (It's ok, maths is fun, copy/pasting is not.)

    He promoted his results in Volume 1 of his book The Art of Computer Programming.

    Four years later, Dennis Ritchie ignored them.

    C++'s  std::fmod  is still using $ \trunc $, while  std::remainder  uses $ \round $.

    (Donald Knuth's book is not open source, and I don't own it;
    this is Wikipedia hear-say — stay critical.)

    The modulo function removes from its variable whatever exceeds any integer multiple of its modulus.

    Which is not problematic as long as everyone remains positive...

    Say your modulus is 5.
    Then:

    $$ 6 \;=\; 1\times5 \,+\, 1 \quad\implies\quad \MOD(6 \mathrel{,} 5) \;=\; 1 $$

    or:

    $$ 102 \;=\; 20\times5 \,+\, 2 \quad\implies\quad \MOD(102 \mathrel{,} 5) \;=\; 2 $$

    or:

    $$ 35 \;=\; 7\times5 \,+\, 0 \quad\implies\quad \MOD(35 \mathrel{,} 5) \;=\; 0 $$

    Now what about $ -13 $?

    Is it $ \; -2\times5 - 3 \; $ or $ \; -3\times5 + 2 \; $?

    The key word is integer multiple.

    If $ a $ is an integer multiple of $ b $, then $ \; a/b \,=\, n \; $ is an integer.

    Then, if $ \; d \,=\, a + c \; $, the modulo function should output $ c $,
    assuming $ d $ is the variable, $ b $ is the modulus, $ \; c \,\lt\, b \; $, and all terms are (temporarily) positive.

    So we want:

    $$ \MOD(d \mathrel{,} b) \;=\; c \;=\; d \,-\, a \;=\; d \,-\, b\cdot a/b \;=\; d \,-\, b\cdot n $$

    Except: how do we extract $ a $?

    All we know is $ b $ and $ d $.

    How about:

    $$ d/b \;=\; \frac{a + c}{b} \;=\; a/b \,+\, c/b \;=\; n \,+\, c/b $$ $$ c \;\lt\; b \quad\iff\quad c/b \;\lt\; 1 $$

    So $ n $ is the integer part of $ \; d/b \; $.

    And if we find $ n $, we get $ \ b\cdot n \; $, which, subtracted from $ d $, gives $ c $.

    Four candidates.

    Let's try them on positive numbers first.

    Let's say the modulus is $ 3 $ and the variable is $ 4.8 $.

    We should get $ \; c \,=\, 1.8 \; $.

    $$ \ceil(4.8/3) \;=\; \ceil(1.6) \;=\; 2 $$ $$ 2\times3 \;=\; 6 $$ $$ 4.8 \,-\, 6 \;=\; -1.2 \;\neq\; 1.8 $$ $$ \round(4.8/3) \;=\; \round(1.6) \;=\; 2 $$ $$ 2\times3 \;=\; 6 $$ $$ 4.8 \,-\, 6 \;=\; -1.2 \;\neq\; 1.8 $$ $$ \trunc(4.8/3) \;=\; \trunc(1.6) \;=\; 1 $$ $$ 1\times3 \;=\; 3 $$ $$ 4.8 \,-\, 3 \;=\; 1.8 $$ $$ \floor(4.8/3) \;=\; \floor(1.6) \;=\; 1 $$ $$ 1\times3 \;=\; 3 $$ $$ 4.8 \,-\, 3 \;=\; 1.8 $$

    Two candidates: $ \trunc $ and $ \floor $.

    The next logical step would be to discriminate them against a negative variable.

    But what exactly do we expect from a negative variable?

    Let's invent a problem.

    We're three children selling lemonade to buy cigarettes and beer.

    At the end of the day, we split the earnings democratically (3 parts) and what's left (the remainder) goes to the treasury.

    So the earnings are the variable and the modulus is 3.

    Now let's say it's been a shitty day: zero customer and 7€ invested in lemons; we share a debt: $ -7 $.

    So: $ \; \MOD(-7 \mathrel{,} 3) \; $.

    $$ -7/3 \;=\; -2 \,-\, 1/3 $$

    $ \floor $ gives $ -3 $, $ \trunc $ gives $ -2 $.

    $$ -7 \,-\, -3\times3 \;=\; 9 \,-\, 7 \;=\; 2 $$ $$ -7 \,-\, -2\times3 \;=\; 6 \,-\, 7 \;=\; -1 $$

    If we use $ \trunc $, now the treasury has a debt...

    Maybe that's what we want?

    There are no bad tools, as long as they're appropriately used.

    Let's invent a problem where we do know what we want.

    You're circuit-bending a Mellotron.

    The Mellotron is the first sampler ever if you ignore the Chamberlin and whatever Pierre Schaeffer was doing in the 1930's.

    The samples were stored on magnetic tapes and played on key-press.

    It would have been the perfect drone machine if only the samples had been longer than 8 seconds, or better yet: loopable.

    If you've never heard of it, you can try listening to an underground garage band from Liverpool called the Beatles.

    You're not into psychedelic pop?

    Electronic music?
    $ \!\longrightarrow\quad $ Jean-Michel Jarre (Maurice's son, yes)
    Progressive rock?
    $ \!\longrightarrow\quad $ King Crimson
    Death metal?
    $ \!\longrightarrow\quad $ Opeth
    Hip-hop?
    $ \!\longrightarrow\quad $ pretty much anything LoFi

    You want the tapes to play infinitely.

    You take the flute low E, cut the attack and join both ends of the piece of tape.

    Say the tape's length is 1. So is the playback speed.

    So when you press the low E on the keyboard during a time of 1, you're back at the beginning of the loop.

    Any integer number of full loops so far is irrelevant regarding your current position on the tape.

    Except, woops: you've wired the rotor in reverse: the tape is playing backwards.

    So, e.g., if you press the key during 0.4, you've moved by $ - $0.4 on the tape.
    Which is equivalent to $ + $0.6.

    Let's name variables.

    • $ L \,=\, 1 \; $ is the length of the looped magnetic tape;
    • $ T \,=\, 1 \; $ is the time required to play the full loop once;
    • $ \f \,=\, -L/T \,=\, -1 \; $ is the rotor's speed;
    • $ t $ is how long you've pressed the key;
    • $ l(t) \,=\, \f t \,=\, -t \; $ is the tape length covered backwards at the end of $ t $;

    let's temporarily assume that $ l(0) $ always resets to zero (or equivalently: that you've only pressed the key once).

    We want $ \lambda(t) $, the position on the tape.

    It should always be positive and less than $ \; L \,=\, 1 \; $.

    What we know:

    $$ l(t) \;=\; -(n + \delta)L \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta $$ $$ n \;\in\; \mathbb{N}_{\geq0} $$ $$ 0 \;\leq\; \delta \;\lt\; 1 $$

    What we want:

    $$ \lambda(t) \;\in\; \left[ 0 \mathrel{,} L \right[ \;=\; \left[ 0 \mathrel{,} 1 \right[ $$ $$ \delta \;=\; 0 \quad\implies\quad \lambda(t) \;=\; \lambda(n) \;=\; 0 $$ $$ \delta \;\neq\; 0 \quad\implies\quad \lambda(t) \;=\; L \,-\, \delta \;=\; 1 \,-\, \delta $$

    Our modulus is $ \; L \,=\, 1 \; $.
    Our variable is $ \; -t \;=\; l(t) \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta \; $.

    Using $ \trunc $

    Case 1: $ \; \delta \,=\, 0 \; $
    $$ -t \,-\, T\cdot\trunc(-t/T) \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta \,-\, \trunc(-n \,-\, \delta) $$ $$ =\; -n \,-\, \trunc(-n) \;=\; -n \,-\, -n \;=\; 0 $$

    Ok.

    Case 2: $ \; \delta \,\neq\, 0 \; $
    $$ -t \,-\, T\cdot\trunc(-t/T) \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta \,-\, \trunc(-n \,-\, \delta) $$ $$ =\; -n \,-\, \delta \,-\, -n \;=\; -\delta $$

    Nope.

    Using $ \floor $

    Case 1: $ \; \delta \,=\, 0 \; $
    $$ -t \,-\, T\cdot\floor(-t/T) \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta \,-\, \floor(-n \,-\, \delta) $$ $$ =\; -n \,-\, \floor(-n) \;=\; -n \,-\, -n \;=\; 0 $$

    Ok.

    Case 2: $ \; \delta \,\neq\, 0 \; $
    $$ -t \,-\, T\cdot\floor(-t/T) \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta \,-\, \floor(-n \,-\, \delta) $$ $$ =\; -n \,-\, \delta \,-\, (-n - 1) \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta \,+\, n \,+\, 1 $$ $$ =\; 1 \,-\, \delta \;=\; L \,-\, \delta $$

    Ok.

    $ \floor $ wins.

    Let's clean that up.

    We can drop the length variables and leave $ T $ undefined.

    $$ -t \;=\; -nT \,-\, \delta T \quad\iff\quad -t/T \;=\; -n \,-\, \delta $$ $$ n \;\in\; \mathbb{N}_{\geq0} $$ $$ 0 \:\lt\: \delta \:\lt\: 1 $$

    and we want $ \; T \,-\, \delta T \; $.

    $$ \floor(-t/T) \;=\; \floor(-n \,-\, \delta) \;=\; -n \,-\, 1 $$ $$ \implies\quad T\cdot\floor(-t/T) \;=\; -nT \,-\, T \;=\; -t \,+\, \delta T \,-\, T $$ $$ \iff\quad T \,-\, \delta T \;=\; -t \,-\, T\cdot\floor(-t/T) $$

    Ok, so we have our general formula:

    $$ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} y) \;=\; x \,-\, y\cdot\floor(x/y) $$

    Notice that the division by $ y $ implies that a modulus of zero outputs  <undefined>  or  NaN  or  [SYNTAX_ERROR]  &c. for all $ x $.

    Alternative notation:

    $$ x \mod y \;=\; \MOD(x \mathrel{,} y) $$

    Technically: left version is regular, and right version is code-friendly.

    I use both interchangeably depending on which one is clearer in context.

    Regular version is better at identifying which variable plays which role;
    dev version can be cleaner when mixed with other terms and nested parenthesis.

    What does it look like on a graph?

    Let's re-normalise the modulus to 1.

    Then:

    $$ x \mod 1 \;=\; x \,-\, \floor(x) $$ $$ =\; \INT(x) \,+\, \DEC(x) \;-\; \Bigl[\; \INT(x) \,+\, \min\Bigl( \sgn\bigl( \DEC(x) \bigr) \mathrel{,} 0 \Bigr) \;\Bigl] $$ $$ =\; \INT(x) \,+\, \DEC(x) \,-\, \INT(x) \,-\, \min\Bigl( \sgn\bigl( \DEC(x) \bigr) \,,\ 0 \Bigr) $$ $$ =\; \DEC(x) \;-\; \left\{\; \begin{alignedat}{2} 0 & \quad\text{if}\quad \DEC(x) \:\gt\: 0 \\ 0 & \quad\text{if}\quad \DEC(x) \: = \: 0 \\ -1 & \quad\text{if}\quad \DEC(x) \:\lt\: 0 \end{alignedat} \right. $$ $$ x \;\geq\; 0 \quad\implies\quad 0 \;\leq\; \DEC(x) \;\lt\; 1 $$ $$ x \;\lt\; 0 \quad\implies\quad -1 \;\lt\; \DEC(x) \;\lt\; 0 $$ $$ \hphantom{x \;\lt\; 0} \quad\iff\quad 0 \;\lt\; \DEC(x) \,+\, 1 \;\lt\; 1 $$

    The range is: $ \; \left[0 \mathrel{,} 1\right[ \; $.

    Next:

    $$ a \;\in\; \pm\mathbb{N} $$ $$ a \;\lt\; b \;\lt\; a \,+\, 1 \quad\implies\quad \floor(a) \;=\; \floor(b) \;=\; a $$ $$ \implies\quad a \,-\, \floor(a) \;\lt\; b \,-\, \floor(b) $$

    It's strictly increasing between integers.

    Next:

    $$ a \;\lt\; ka \;\lt\; a \,+\, 1 \quad\implies\quad \floor(ka) \;=\; \floor(a) \;=\; a $$ $$ iff\quad ka \,-\, \floor(ka) \;=\; ka \,-\, a $$ $$ \implies\quad \frac{d}{dka} \MOD(ka \mathrel{,} 1) \;=\; 1 $$

    It's linear between integers.

    Or you could just plot it on a graphing calculator app.

    But remember: once the crypto-fascists and their robocops take over, those who depend on digital tech will die first.

    Now, what if the modulus is not normalised?

    Let's use a more general approach: let's compare two proportional moduli.

    $$ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} y) \;=\; x \,-\, y\cdot\floor(x/y) $$ $$ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} ky) \;=\; x \,-\, ky\cdot\floor(x/ky) $$ $$ \hphantom{\MOD(x \mathrel{,} ky)} \;=\; k\left( x/k \,-\, y\cdot \floor\!\left( \frac{x/k}{y} \right) \right) $$ $$ \hphantom{\MOD(x \mathrel{,} ky)} \;=\; k\cdot\MOD(x/k \mathrel{,} y) $$

    If $ \; y \,=\, 1 \; $:

    $$ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} k) \;=\; k\cdot\MOD(x/k \mathrel{,} 1) $$

    So the unit-modulus is the basic template of all moduli.

    But more interestingly: the modulus can be factored out of the modulo function.

    Curve-wise?

    You might recognise the Working-class trigonometric deform;
    specifically: the full isometric special case, where phase and DC offset are null, and amplitude and period are equal.

    $$ f(x) \;=\; \MOD(x \mathrel{,} 1) $$ $$ \implies\quad \MOD(x \mathrel{,} y) \;=\; \mathcal{WCTD} \bigl\{\, f(x) \,\bigr\} ( 0 \mathrel{,} y \mathrel{,} y \mathrel{,} 0 ) $$

    So the curves always have the same shape, the modulus is basically a zoom factor.
    A non-deforming one.

    Of course we can deform the curve like with any function.
    Just: always under complete control. Isn't that convenient?

    And about the range: $ \; \MOD(\xi \mathrel{,} 1) \; $ is in $ \; \left[0 \mathrel{,} 1\right[ \; $,
    if $ \; \xi \,=\, x/k \; $, then $ \; \MOD(x/k \mathrel{,} 1) \; $is in $ \; \left[0 \mathrel{,} 1\right[ \; $.

    Since the function is linear and increasing between discontinuities,
    $ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} k) \:=\: k\cdot\MOD(\xi \mathrel{,} 1) \; $ is in $ \; k\cdot\left[0 \mathrel{,} 1\right[ \;=\; \left[0 \mathrel{,} k\right[ \; $.

    The whole point all along

    What about negative moduli?

    Mathematically, no aporia: factor out the negative modulus,
    reach the factored normalised form (I guess it's its name now),
    you get a positive unit-modulus, a sign-flipped variable and a negative factor, which flips the range.

    So:

    $$ k \;\lt\; 0 \implies\quad \MOD(x \mathrel{,} k) \;=\; k\cdot\MOD(x/k \mathrel{,} 1) \;\in\; \left] k \mathrel{,} 0 \right] $$

    Ok, so it's always negative — nothing a constant term can't fix;
    but more interestingly: the upper bound is now included, and the lower one excluded.

    So, e.g., $ \; \MOD(x \mathrel{,} —k) \,+\, k \; $ (with $ k $ positive) can reach $ k $ and is never zero,
    the roles are flipped, and everything else remains identical...

    Unless flipping everything also made the function decrease between discontinuities.

    Let's check:

    $$ k \;\lt\; 0 \quad\iff\quad \sgn(k) \;=\; -1 $$ $$ \iff\quad \left| k \right|\sgn(k) \;=\; \left| k \right|(-1) \;=\; -\left| k \right| \;=\; k $$ $$ \implies\; \left\{ \begin{alignedat}{2} a/k & \;=\; -a/\left| k \right| \\ b/k & \;=\; -b/\left| k \right| \end{alignedat} \right. $$ $$ a/k \;\lt\; b/k \;\lt\; a/k \,+\, 1 \quad\iff\quad -a/\left| k \right| \;\lt\; -b/\left| k \right| \;\lt\; -a/\left| k \right| \,+\, 1 $$ $$ \iff\quad -a \;\lt\; -b \;\lt\; -a \,+\, \left| k \right| \quad\iff\quad a \;\gt\; b \;\gt\; a \,-\, \left| k \right| $$ $$ a/k \;\in\; \pm\mathbb{N} \quad\implies\quad \floor(b/k) \;=\; \floor(a/k) \;=\; a/k \;=\; -a/\left| k \right| $$ $$ \MOD(a \mathrel{,} k) \;=\; k\bigl( a/k \,-\, \floor(a/k) \bigr) $$ $$ \hphantom{ \MOD(a \mathrel{,} k) } \;=\; a \,-\, k \, \frac{-a}{ \left| k \right| } \;=\; a \,-\ \sgn(k)(-a) $$ $$ \hphantom{ \MOD(a \mathrel{,} k) } \;=\; a \,-\, (-1)(-a) \;=\; a \,-\, a \;=\; 0 $$ $$ \MOD(b \mathrel{,} k) \;=\; k\bigl( b/k \,-\, \floor(b/k) \bigr) $$ $$ \hphantom{ \MOD(b \mathrel{,} k) } \;=\; b \,-\, k \, \frac{-a}{ \left| k \right| } \;=\; b \,-\ \sgn(k)(-a) $$ $$ \hphantom{ \MOD(b \mathrel{,} k) } \;=\; b \,-\, (-1)(-a) \;=\; b \,-\, a $$ $$ a \;\gt\; b \quad\iff\quad 0 \;\gt\; b \,-\, a $$

    In short:

    $$ a \;\gt\; b \quad\iff\quad \MOD(a \mathrel{,} k) \;\gt\; \MOD(b \mathrel{,} k) $$

    Generalisation:

    $$ b/k \;\lt\; c/k \;\lt\; a/k \,+\, 1 \;\left\{ \begin{alignedat}{1} & \iff\quad b \;\gt\; c \;\gt\; a \,-\, \left| k \right| \\[0.25em] & \implies\quad \floor(c/k) \;=\; \floor(a/k) \;=\; -a/\left| k \right| \end{alignedat} \right. $$ $$ \MOD(c \mathrel{,} k) \;=\; k\bigl( c/k \,-\, \floor(c/k) \bigr) $$ $$ \hphantom{ \MOD(c \mathrel{,} k) } \;=\; c \,-\, k \, \frac{-a}{ \left| k \right| } \;=\; c \,-\, (-1)(-a) \;=\; c \,-\, a $$ $$ b \;\gt\; c \quad\iff\quad b \,-\, a \;\gt\; c \,-\, a $$

    In short:

    $$ b \;\gt\; c \quad\iff\quad \MOD(b \mathrel{,} k) \;\gt\; \MOD(c \mathrel{,} k) $$

    Strictly increasing between discontinuities.

    If you need a decreasing ramp, choose any modulus (positive or negative), then reverse the variable:

    $$ b \;\gt\; c \quad\iff\quad -b \;\lt\; -c $$ $$ \implies\quad \MOD(-b \mathrel{,} k) \;\lt\; \MOD(-c \mathrel{,} k) $$

    Say we're chaining filters.

    First stage:

    $$ Y_1(s) \;=\; H_1(s)X(s) $$

    Second stage:

    $$ Y_2(s) \;=\; H_2(s)Y_1(s) \;=\; H_2(s)H_1(s)X(s) $$

    Third stage:

    $$ Y_3(s) \;=\; H_3(s)Y_2(s) \;=\; H_3(s)H_2(s)H_1(s)X(s) $$

    $ N $th stage:

    $$ Y_N(s) \;=\; X(s) \prod_{k=1}^N H_k(s) $$

    Assuming $ X(s) $ has its phase normalised to zero,
    then $ Y_N(s) $'s phase is:

    $$ \phi \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^N \arg\bigl( H_k(s) \bigr) $$

    This sum could very easily exceed the range $ \; \left]-\pi \mathrel{,} +\pi\right] \; $.
    (The probability grows with $ N $.)

    Oh, no: a zero-crossing modulus that reaches its upper bound!
    If only we'd just spent hundreds of lines preparing for this...

    We've found two possible modulo functions:

    • positive modulus: ranges from zero (included) to the modulus (excluded)
    • negative modulus: ranges from the modulus (excluded) to zero (included)

    We never cross zero as is.

    Two options:

    1. use a positive modulus and negative DC offset;
    2. use a negative modulus and a positive DC offset.

    Since we want to include the upper bound and exclude the lower one, we want a negative modulus.

    Let's forget about $ \pi $ and unit-normalise as usual.

    We want the range: $ \; \left]-1 \mathrel{,} +1\right] \; $.

    $ 1 \,-\, -1 \;=\; 2 \; $ so that's our span, the absolute value of our modulus.

    We want it negative so $ -2 $.

    $ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} -2) \; $ is in $ \; \left]-2 \mathrel{,} 0\right] \; $ so it needs a DC offset of $ +1 $.

    So:

    $$ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} 2) \,+\, 1 \;\in\; \left] -1 \mathrel{,} +1 \right] $$

    Whatever happens to $ x $ won't affect this range:
    that's how modulo works: anything from outside goes back inside.

    Ok so we have our modulus.

    Curve-wise?

    $$ \MOD(x \mathrel{,} 2) \,+\, 1 \;=\; -2\cdot\MOD\bigl(x/(-2) \mathrel{,} 1) \,+\, 1 $$

    That's the isometric case of the trigonometric deform (plus a DC offset).

    Meaning:

    $$ \alpha\cdot f\left( \frac{ x - x_0 }{T} \,+\, DC \right) $$

    with $ \; \alpha \,=\, T \,=\, -2 \; $, $ \; x_0 \,=\, 0 \; $ and $ \; DC \,=\, 1 \; $.

    So the curve is not deformed: it's isometrically re-scaled and vertically offset by $ +1 $.

    So the worse that could have happened is a horizontal offset ($ x_0 $).

    Meaning: if we know one expected output value and one current output value, we can compare them and get the potentially required $ x_0 $.

    Let's go unoriginal: we want zero at zero.

    $$ \MOD(0 \mathrel{,} -2) \,+\, 1 \;=\; 0 \,-\, (-2)\floor\bigl( 0/(-2) \bigr) \,+\, 1 $$ $$ \hphantom{ \MOD(0 \mathrel{,} -2) \,+\, 1 } \;=\; 0 \,-\, 0 \,+\, 1 \;=\; 1 $$ $$ 1 \,-\, x_0 \;=\; 0 \quad\iff\quad \MOD(x \,-\, 1 \mathrel{,} -2) \,+\, 1 $$

    Meet the Working-class modulus fix:

    $$ M_1(x) \;=\; \MOD(x \,-\, \mathrel{,} -2) \,+\, 1 $$

    Since moduli can be factored out, they can be factored in.

    This is a template.

    The 1-index represents the absolute value of the range's boundaries.

    General formula:

    $$ M_k(x) \;=\; k\cdot M_1(x/k) \;\in\; \left] -k \mathrel{,} +k \right] $$

    You can flip the boundaries' exclusion/inclusion by simply choosing a negative $ k $.

    And regarding phase:

    $$ M_\pi(\phi) \;=\; \pi \Bigl(\: \MOD\bigl(\phi/2 \,-\, 1 \mathrel{,} -2\bigr) \,+\, 1 \:\Bigr) $$

    $ \phi/\pi $ can look like a division at first glance, but very often, we get our values in radians as $ 2\pi $ times something, then it reads as ignore $ \pi $ rather than divide by it.

    This function does not replace atan2.
